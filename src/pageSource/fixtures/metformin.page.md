Metformin is a biguanide that lowers blood glucose in [[type 2 diabetes]]. It reduces hepatic glucose production, decreases intestinal absorption of glucose and improves insulin sensitivity, while insulin secretion stays unchanged, so on its own it rarely causes hypoglycemia.[@dailymed:1ed9dde4-339c-486f-a346-dde33a5e493f] It is inexpensive and has decades of outcome data: in overweight patients in UKPDS 34 it reduced diabetes-related end points and all-cause mortality compared with conventional therapy.[@pmid:9742977]

Guidelines now choose first-line therapy by comorbidity. In people with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, heart failure or chronic kidney disease, a [[GLP-1 receptor agonist]] or [[SGLT2 inhibitor]] with proven benefit is recommended whether or not metformin is used; otherwise metformin remains a common first choice, especially when cost matters.[@pmid:39651989] In the Diabetes Prevention Program, metformin reduced progression from [[prediabetes]] to diabetes compared with placebo.[@pmid:11832527]

## Mechanism of action

The main glucose-lowering effect is reduced hepatic gluconeogenesis. How metformin does this at clinical concentrations is still debated: complex I inhibition and AMPK activation are seen mainly at supra-pharmacological concentrations, and a redox-dependent mechanism has more support at clinically relevant ones.[@pmid:32897388]

| Immediate-release tablets (age 10 and older) | 500 mg twice daily, with meals | 500 mg weekly | 2,000 mg/day, divided twice daily |
| Extended-release tablets (adults) | 500 mg once daily with the evening meal | 500 mg every 1–2 weeks | 2,000 mg once daily |

Sources: immediate-release[@dailymed:b6d7edc9-93d6-4480-906d-55c18d053600]; extended-release.[@dailymed:1ed9dde4-339c-486f-a346-dde33a5e493f]

- Doses above 2,000 mg/day of immediate-release may be better tolerated given three times a day with meals.[@dailymed:b6d7edc9-93d6-4480-906d-55c18d053600]
- Patients on immediate-release can switch to extended-release once daily at the same total daily dose, up to 2,000 mg.[@dailymed:1ed9dde4-339c-486f-a346-dde33a5e493f]
- Extended-release tablets must be swallowed whole ({{do_not_crush}}); their inactive ingredients may pass in the stool as a soft mass that looks like the tablet.[@dailymed:1ed9dde4-339c-486f-a346-dde33a5e493f]

## Renal dosing

Check eGFR before starting and at least once a year; more often in people at risk of declining kidney function, such as older adults.[@dailymed:1ed9dde4-339c-486f-a346-dde33a5e493f]

:::renal-dosing
egfr < 30: contraindicated; stop if eGFR falls below 30 [@dailymed:1ed9dde4-339c-486f-a346-dde33a5e493f][@dailymed:b6d7edc9-93d6-4480-906d-55c18d053600]
egfr 30-45: do not start; if already taking, assess the benefit and risk of continuing [@dailymed:1ed9dde4-339c-486f-a346-dde33a5e493f][@dailymed:b6d7edc9-93d6-4480-906d-55c18d053600]
egfr >= 45: no labelled dose adjustment; keep monitoring eGFR at least annually [@dailymed:1ed9dde4-339c-486f-a346-dde33a5e493f]
:::

**Iodinated contrast.** Stop metformin at the time of, or before, an iodinated contrast procedure in patients with eGFR 30–60, a history of liver disease, alcoholism or heart failure, or who will receive intra-arterial contrast. Recheck eGFR 48 hours afterwards and restart if kidney function is stable.[@dailymed:b6d7edc9-93d6-4480-906d-55c18d053600]

## Safety

- **Lactic acidosis** (boxed warning). Rare but can be fatal. Risk factors: renal impairment, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, age 65 or older, contrast studies, surgery, hypoxic states such as acute heart failure, excessive alcohol and hepatic impairment. If suspected, stop metformin and start supportive care in hospital; prompt hemodialysis is recommended.[@dailymed:1ed9dde4-339c-486f-a346-dde33a5e493f] A Cochrane review of 347 trials and cohorts found no cases in 70,490 patient-years of metformin use and no excess over other glucose-lowering drugs.[@pmid:20393934]
- **Vitamin B12 deficiency.** Metformin can lower B12; the label advises hematologic monitoring every year and B12 every 2 to 3 years.[@dailymed:1ed9dde4-339c-486f-a346-dde33a5e493f] In the Diabetes Prevention Program Outcomes Study, each year of metformin use raised the odds of B12 deficiency (OR 1.13 per year).[@pmid:26900641] See [[vitamin B12 deficiency]].
- **Hypoglycemia** occurs mainly with [[insulin]] or an insulin secretagogue such as a [[sulfonylurea]]; a lower dose of those may be needed.[@dailymed:1ed9dde4-339c-486f-a346-dde33a5e493f]
- **Gastrointestinal effects.** Diarrhea and nausea are the most common adverse reactions.[@dailymed:1ed9dde4-339c-486f-a346-dde33a5e493f]

## Interactions

- **Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors** ([[topiramate]], [[zonisamide]], [[acetazolamide]]) may increase the risk of lactic acidosis; consider more frequent monitoring.
- **Drugs that reduce metformin clearance** ([[ranolazine]], [[vandetanib]], [[dolutegravir]], [[cimetidine]]) may increase metformin accumulation; weigh benefits and risks.
- **Alcohol** potentiates metformin's effect on lactate metabolism; warn against excessive intake.

All three from the drug interactions section of the label.[@dailymed:1ed9dde4-339c-486f-a346-dde33a5e493f]

## Pregnancy and lactation

Published studies have not shown a clear association between metformin and major birth defects, miscarriage or adverse maternal or fetal outcomes, although methodological limits mean risk can't be ruled out. Poorly controlled diabetes in pregnancy carries its own risks. Metformin is present in human milk; infant doses are estimated at 0.11% to 1% of the maternal weight-adjusted dose.[@dailymed:1ed9dde4-339c-486f-a346-dde33a5e493f]

Metformin may cause ovulation in some anovulatory premenopausal women, so discuss the possibility of unintended pregnancy.[@dailymed:1ed9dde4-339c-486f-a346-dde33a5e493f]

## Patient education

- Take it with meals (extended-release: with the evening meal). Diarrhea and nausea are the most common side effects; starting low and increasing the dose slowly helps you tolerate it.
- Swallow extended-release tablets whole. Don't crush, cut or chew them.
- Stop taking it and get medical help right away if you feel very weak or tired, have unusual muscle pain, trouble breathing, unusual sleepiness, stomach problems with nausea, vomiting or diarrhea, feel cold, dizzy or light-headed, or have a slow or irregular heartbeat. These can be signs of lactic acidosis.
- Avoid drinking a lot of alcohol, including binge drinking.
- Tell your care team you take metformin before surgery or any X-ray or scan that uses contrast dye; you may need to pause it.
- You'll need regular blood tests for kidney function and blood counts.

Counselling points from the FDA patient information for metformin extended-release tablets.[@dailymed:1ed9dde4-339c-486f-a346-dde33a5e493f]
