# This is Our Commons: 

## The Case for Open Pharmaceutical Knowledge

**September 2026
Joshua Semock, PharmD · pharmacycommons.org**

Drug information is paradoxically both abundant and scarce. Anyone can open a browser, search for drug information across public and federal databases, and end up lost in a labyrinth of information or go down a rabbit hole on Reddit. Either way the clinical picture quickly disintegrates. This is why pharmacies, universities, hospitals, and sometimes individuals out of pocket pay for curated information systems to keep their facilities running, what used to be available to own in a book. Three major conglomerates, Micromedex, UpToDate/Lexicomp, and ClinicalKey, captured this market vacancy. However, what they took from the commons into the marketplace was the commodification of our collective knowledge. Constructing paywalls, an enclosure, separating the ecosystem of knowledge from the laborer, requiring an employer to pay for access. 

To John Locke, the seventeenth-century English physician and philosopher, private enclosure seemed rational: when human labor mixes with the uncultivated commons, it becomes private property. Yet in modern healthcare information, that social contract is completely inverted. The curated knowledge base does not become the property of the clinical laborers who produced, reviewed, or synthesized it. It becomes the intellectual property of a corporate intermediary selling a subscription plan. Physicians, nurses, pharmacists, and many other practitioners already paid for this knowledge once, often going hundreds of thousands of dollars into educational debt to earn the requirements for licensure.

The core issue is not that a private publisher organizes information cleanly for use. The issue is that these entities claim exclusive proprietary control over the distribution of knowledge they never generated. Advances in pharmacotherapy do not arise from corporate boardrooms; they emerge from public universities, governmental grants, bedside clinical trials, and collective human struggle. In Modern Monetary Theory, the public funds the infrastructure and discovery of medical science, while the collective sale of our labor power anchors the purchasing power of the currency that backs these grants. We, the public, have already financed the discovery, testing, and validation of pharmacotherapy. We therefore possess an inherent right to access that knowledge freely.

Instead, we pay for this corporate enclosure across every facet of our lives unknowingly: through suppressed healthcare wages, bloated hospital overhead, higher insurance premiums, and now through closed API and AI subscriptions. The enclosure has also created a dangerous technical vulnerability. Large language models and point-of-care clinical tools are ravenous for structured data. When proprietary silos wall off authoritative, primary source clinical evidence, chat models hallucinate because they can't read it. These are not merely technical bugs; they are structural symptoms of paywalled and unstructured medical information. What it results in ultimately is the inability of the patient to fact check their provider.

>"The capacity of individuals to sustain cooperating sets of rules is far greater than standard economic theories of rational, self-interested individuals indicate." – Elinor Ostrom

Initiatives like Wikipedia and Open Evidence have demonstrated the power of shared references, but the soil of the clinical commons requires deeper, collective cultivation. A unified, machine readable, fully open pharmaceutical knowledge base does not yet exist simply because it defies the extractive incentives of modern health care capital. Something like Pharmacy Commons doesn't exist yet because of that capitalist imperative, a true tragedy. We know the myth of "the tragedy of the commons" is wholly preventable when communities work together without privatization. Elinor Ostrom, who won the 2009 Nobel Prize in Economics, showed that local communities can successfully manage shared resources together without state control or privatization.
